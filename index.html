How Cp and Cpk are calculated

Cp: can the spread fit?

Cp = USL − LSL6σ

Compares the allowed width to the process width (±3σ = 6σ). It ignores where the process is centered, so it tells you the best Cpk you could get if the mean sat exactly in the middle.

Cpk: does it fit where it actually sits?

Cpk = min ( USL − μ3σ , μ − LSL3σ )

Measures the distance from the mean to the nearer spec limit, in units of 3σ. Cpk = 1 means the nearest limit is exactly 3σ away. Cpk is never larger than Cp; the gap between them shows how far off-center the process is.

Common interpretation

These cutoffs are conventions, not laws of nature. Textbooks, industries, and individual customers set them differently, and every one of them assumes a normal, stable process.

Below 1.00Not capable. Some parts are already out of spec.
1.00 to 1.33Marginal. It fits, with little room for drift.
1.33 to 1.67Capable. 1.33 is a common minimum for existing processes [1]. In the automotive industry, values in this range are often accepted only after the customer reviews them.
1.67 and upHighly capable. A typical target for safety-critical characteristics on new processes [1], and a common acceptance level for new parts in the automotive industry. 2.00 corresponds to Six Sigma quality [1].

What a Cpk number actually tells you

Cpk is a distance: how many standard deviations of room the process has before it reaches the nearer spec limit (3 × Cpk). That distance converts directly into how often a bad part is made. Whether that rate is acceptable depends on what one bad part costs, and that is a business decision, not a statistics rule.

0.67, 1.33 and 1.67 are the rounded values of 2/3, 4/3 and 5/3, which is why they correspond to exactly 2σ, 4σ and 5σ. Counts are for the nearer side only. If the process is centered, both sides contribute equally, so double them. Values assume a normal, stable process.

Discussion: what Cpk would you require?

For each case, decide what Cpk you would put in the purchase contract, then open the discussion points. Try the numbers in the simulator above.

You are outsourcing 16d common nails, the standard 3½-inch framing nail used in U.S. house construction, bought by the millions. What Cpk would you mandate?

A single bad nail costs almost nothing. It is easy to see, the user simply picks another one, and nobody gets hurt. At Cpk = 1.00 roughly 1 nail in 740 is out of spec on the near side; at 1.33 it is about 1 in 32,000. Many buyers would accept something around 1.0 to 1.33 here.

Demanding 1.67 or 2.00 would force the supplier onto tighter equipment and more inspection, and the price per nail would go up for almost no benefit to the customer. The better question is which characteristic matters: a shank that is too thick splits the lumber, and a nail that is too short does not hold the framing together.

A component goes into a surgical kit, for example the diameter of a suture needle or the locking mechanism of a clamp. What Cpk would you require?

Here a single defect can harm a patient, and it may not be noticed until the moment of use. If you make 50,000 kits a year, Cpk = 1.33 means about 1 to 2 bad kits every year; Cpk = 1.67 means roughly one every 70 years.

Most companies would require at least 1.67 for a characteristic like this, often 2.00, and would still add inspection or testing. The required number follows from the consequence of failure, not from a textbook table.

You buy a safety part for a car's brakes or airbag. What does the automotive industry typically expect?

In the automotive industry, suppliers of new parts are commonly expected to show a capability index of at least 1.67 for important characteristics. Values between 1.33 and 1.67 may be accepted after the customer reviews the study, and values below 1.33 usually require a corrective action plan. Each carmaker writes its own exact requirements into its supplier agreements.

This is a good example of a cutoff written into a contract: the number is agreed between buyer and supplier before production starts.

A meat packer fills packages labeled 1 lb. Is a high Cpk always better here?

Underweight packages break labeling law and upset customers, so the lower side matters a lot. Overweight packages are legal but give product away. The company could raise Cpk on the lower side by simply filling everything heavier, but every extra ounce is lost money.

The smarter fix is reducing variation (raising Cp), which lets the company move the mean closer to 1 lb without producing underweight packages. The "right" Cpk is a balance between legal risk and the cost of giveaway.

A supplier reports Cp = 2.00 but Cpk = 1.00. Do you reject the parts?

Not necessarily. Cp = 2.00 says the process is very precise: its ±3σ spread uses only half the tolerance. Cpk = 1.00 says the mean has drifted so far toward one limit that it has only 3σ of room on that side. Load the "Very precise but off-center" scenario to see it.

This is usually an easy fix, like a tool offset or machine setting. Re-centering the process would raise Cpk to 2.00 with no new equipment. A reasonable response is to ask for corrective action to re-center, rather than treating the supplier as incapable.

Why not simply require Cpk = 2.00 on every part?

Capability costs money. A higher Cpk means more precise machines, more maintenance, tighter material control, and more measurement. For low-risk characteristics, that cost is passed on in the price without giving the customer anything they value.

Companies usually set higher requirements only for characteristics flagged as critical or safety-related, and accept lower values for cosmetic or non-functional features.

A supplier reports Cpk = 1.35 from 25 parts. Has the process really met a 1.33 requirement?

Not with confidence. Cpk is an estimate from a sample, and with only 25 parts the true value could easily be below 1.33. It also only means something if the process was stable (in control) when the data were collected.

Before trusting the number, check the control chart, look at how many parts were measured, and ask whether the data cover different shifts, operators, and material lots.
